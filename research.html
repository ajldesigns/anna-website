The Borlaug Reform: A Geopolitical Reaction to Global Hunger and the Green Revolution of the 1960s

MA Northeast Region Winner · MA State Winner · National Finalist and 6th Place Overall · White House Honorable Mention

what it was for

A historical paper for National History Day 2026, on that year's theme of revolution, reaction, and reform. I worked from declassified diplomatic records of the 1966-67 Indian famine and interviewed three people: Dr. Mark Montgomery of Grinnell College, former U.S. agriculture secretary Thomas Vilsack, and Mashal Husain of the World Food Prize Foundation.

abstract

This paper argues that the Green Revolution was as much Cold War strategy as scientific breakthrough. India was importing grain to feed its people, and American policymakers read that hunger as a security threat, since a hungry country was one where communism could take hold. Food became leverage, held back under the short tether policy to press India toward market reform. Borlaug's semi-dwarf wheat supplied the technical half, and in the Punjab output rose from 1.9 million tons in 1965 to over 5.6 million by 1972. The revolution was real and deliberate, and America gained influence from it.

2026 · world food prize foundation · icrisat

Inclusive Nutrition-Sensitive Entrepreneurship in India: Journeys, Constraints, and Policy Pathways of Agrifood Enterprises in Andhra Pradesh, Maharashtra, and Telangana

Borlaug-Ruan International Intern, World Food Prize Foundation · ICRISAT, Hyderabad, India

One of 13 interns selected worldwide aged 17 to 20 · report under peer review for publication with the ICRISAT research team

what it was for

I spent eight weeks at ICRISAT in Hyderabad as a Borlaug-Ruan International Intern, one of 13 students worldwide aged 17 to 20 selected by the World Food Prize Foundation. The field team had already finished interviewing 29 agrifood entrepreneurs across three Indian states, so my assignment was to turn those transcripts into 29 founder profiles and a cross-case policy analysis.

abstract

India grows enough grain to fill its warehouses, yet diets still lack the micronutrients that millets once supplied. This report studies the small, mostly women-led enterprises putting millets and other nutrition-dense foods back on Indian plates, through 29 case studies across Andhra Pradesh, Maharashtra, and Telangana. Three patterns emerged. Entry owes more to necessity than to market opportunity, with 18 founders starting after a health crisis, a career break, or a social mission. Scale tracks institutional buyers, since every firm that grew sells mainly to businesses or institutions. And the schemes meant to help are widely known but unevenly delivered: 20 of 29 enterprises had dealt with one, and 15 reported friction.

2026 · icrisat agrifood series · entrepreneur profiles

Entrepreneur Profiles: 29 Biographical Narratives from the ICRISAT Agrifood Series

Andhra Pradesh, Maharashtra, and Telangana · 29 enterprises, written in the founders' own voices

In production for publication, with me listed as a co-author alongside the ICRISAT research team

what it was for

Alongside the report, each of the 29 enterprises also has its own profile: a full narrative of how one founder built one business, written in the founder's own voice. Each opens with a fact box and closes with a third-person summary and an at-a-glance table. I wrote all 29 from the translated transcripts, keeping the speaker's phrasing intact, roughly 2,000 words each.

abstract

The profiles are first-person, because the entrepreneurs' own phrasing carried more than any summary could. Each follows the same arc: why they started, what they had to learn, how customers came around, what the business makes, and where it is headed. Read together, the 29 stories are the evidence behind the report. A dietitian who could not find the healthy food she was prescribing, a computer instructor who turned an idli plant into a jowar mill, a former IBM manager now running seven processing lines. Most are first-generation founders, most are women, and the businesses range from a home kitchen to a company operating in 16 Indian states.

phillips academy · history 300 · spring 2026

The Double V in the Delta: How the Second Great Migration Forged the Foundation of the Civil Rights Movement

History 300, Phillips Academy · Spring 2026 · 3,356 words with an annotated bibliography

what it was for

A final research paper for History 300, on the Second Great Migration and the mechanization of Southern agriculture. I had already written about the 1930s Dust Bowl and the Green Revolution of the 1960s, so the 1940s and 1950s were the gap in between. The paper argues against economic historians who explain the migration as technological displacement.

abstract

Over a million Black Americans served in the Second World War and came home to Jim Crow. This paper argues that the migration that followed was not the passive result of the mechanical cotton picker, as economic historians have long framed it, but a deliberate claim to political self-determination, already ignited by the Double V campaign. Mechanized harvesting went from under 5 percent of the Southern cotton crop in 1950 to over 55 percent by 1960, cutting labor from 130 man-hours per bale to 45. But the migration itself was chosen. Five million Black Southerners moved between 1940 and 1970, and their Northern factory wages funded the legal funds and organizing that carried the movement back South.

phillips academy · scientific computing · fall 2025

Assessing the Relation Between Annual GDP Growth and Inflation Rate Changes in the United States

Scientific Computing, Phillips Academy · Fall 2025

World Bank data for the United States, 1960 to 2024 · Python, pandas, and matplotlib

what it was for

A data analysis project for Scientific Computing, and my first time working end to end in Python. I pulled two World Bank datasets for the United States, annual GDP growth and annual inflation, cleaned and merged them by year, and tested whether growth actually predicts inflation. The project covers 64 years of data, from 1960 to 2024.

abstract

The textbook story is that a growing economy pushes prices up. This project tested that against 64 years of American data and found the relationship far weaker and stranger than the theory suggests. The overall correlation between annual GDP growth and inflation came out at r = -0.153, essentially negligible. A ten-year rolling correlation averaged -0.212 and swung between positive and negative through stagflation, the Great Moderation, and the post-pandemic spike. Only one version of the relationship held up: lag GDP growth by a year and the correlation becomes significant at r = 0.40, p < 0.01. Growth looks less like a real-time signal and more like an accelerator that takes about twelve months to reach consumer prices.

2022 · aapi heritage month essay contest · ames, iowa

AAPI In My Eyes

Ames AAPI Heritage Month Essay Contest, Iowa State University and the Iowa Asian Alliance · 2nd place, statewide

Published by the contest in May 2022 · read on IPTV radio

what it was for

A personal essay for the Ames AAPI Heritage Month essay contest, written while I was in middle school at Johnston. The 2022 theme was AAPI In My Eyes, and the contest was open to middle school, high school, and college students across Iowa. It placed second statewide, and the contest published the winning essays.

abstract

The essay traces how my sense of being Asian American changed. As a child I treated it as a bragging right, being bilingual and having lived in two countries, without understanding what it meant. Then came the pandemic, rising tension with China, and the 2021 shootings in Atlanta, which made my grandmother afraid to walk outside alone. What struck me was what followed: protests worldwide, more than three million dollars raised for the victims' families, and the Iowa City district voting to make Lunar New Year a school holiday. Asian Americans are the fastest-growing group in Iowa, up 72 percent since 2000. The essay ends believing the future looks brighter than it has.
